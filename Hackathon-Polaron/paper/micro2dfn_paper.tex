\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report explains, end to end and in plain language, how we turned scanning
electron microscope (SEM) photographs of a battery electrode into numbers that
describe its internal structure, and then into physics-model estimates of what
that structure might mean for battery behaviour. The dataset is 31 backscattered
electron (BSE) images from three manufacturing batches of a silicon--graphite
anode. The pipeline has five stages: (1)~correct each photograph's uneven
lighting; (2)~colour-code every pixel into one of three materials --- pores,
graphite, silicon --- using one shared set of brightness cut-offs for all
photos; (3)~separate real silicon particles from bright imaging artefacts;
(4)~measure about thirty structural \emph{markers} per image, each with an
uncertainty band; (5)~feed those markers into PyBaMM's Doyle--Fuller--Newman
(DFN) battery model, run repeatedly across the uncertainty ranges, to obtain
consequence \emph{indicators} such as swelling and stress during the cycle.
Every step is deterministic and documented: no machine-learning black box is
used anywhere in the main pipeline, and the measuring rule itself is frozen
once calibrated on the approved reference batch, so arriving photos can never
change how earlier batches were measured.
The honest headline is two-fold. Compositionally, the three batches are
indistinguishable --- about 6\% silicon-candidate material and about 10\%
resolved porosity in each. But we then discovered that the photographs were
taken under at least a dozen distinguishable imaging conditions that line up
with the batch labels almost as well as the material does. After controlling
for that \emph{acquisition} factor, no batch difference in the image set can
currently be asserted --- not because the batches are proven identical, but
because the measurement cannot yet separate material from imaging session.
The pipeline's most defensible outputs are therefore: a measurement system
that knows its own uncertainty, a documented confound that any future
batch-comparison must control for, and a set of candidate signals --- three
unusually bright Batch~1 fields, a within-session-consistent InLens
pore-contrast direction, and a model-conditional silicon stress difference ---
each labelled solid, possible, or can't-tell rather than promoted to a verdict.
All model outputs are reported as ranges and are explicitly labelled as
indicators rather than predictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction --- what problem are we solving?}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{A two-minute battery primer}
A lithium-ion battery cell is a sandwich of two electrode coatings with a
separator in between, all soaked in liquid electrolyte. The electrode we study
here is the \emph{negative electrode} (anode): a thin coating, roughly the
thickness of a human hair, made of microscopic particles glued onto a metal
foil.

The coating contains three things that matter to us:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Graphite particles} --- the workhorse material. When the cell
  charges, lithium ions leave the other electrode, travel through the
  electrolyte, and slot themselves between graphite's atomic layers.
  \item \textbf{Silicon particles} --- a smaller fraction blended in because
  silicon can store roughly ten times more lithium per weight than graphite.
  The catch: silicon swells to about $3\times$ its size when it fills with
  lithium, which stresses everything around it.
  \item \textbf{Pores} --- the empty space between particles. This is where the
  electrolyte lives. Pores are the ``roads'' that lithium ions travel along to
  reach the particles. Their size, shape and connectedness control how fast the
  cell can charge and how evenly it wears.
\end{itemize}

Because performance and lifetime depend on how these ingredients are arranged
--- not just how much of each there is --- two coatings with identical recipes
can behave very differently if they were mixed, coated or calendered
differently. That is why manufacturers photograph polished cross-sections of
electrodes with electron microscopes: the internal arrangement is directly
visible.

\subsection{What this study asks}
We have BSE cross-section images from three manufacturing batches of a
silicon--graphite anode (7, 7 and 17 photographs). We ask:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Do the batches differ structurally, and if so, in which measurable
  features and by how much?
  \item How confident can we be in those differences?
  \item What could the structural differences physically mean, if we feed the
  measured structure into a standard battery physics model?
\end{enumerate}

Just as importantly, we require the answer to be \emph{explainable}: every
number in this report can be traced back to a formula applied to a specific
image, and every claimed difference comes with an uncertainty band.

\subsection{What ``interpretable'' means here}
Much image analysis today hands the problem to a neural network that outputs a
score with no explanation. We deliberately did the opposite. Every stage of the
pipeline is a named, classical method whose behaviour can be inspected:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{r l}
  raw image & $\rightarrow$ lighting correction (Gaussian flat-field)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ pixel classification (shared Otsu thresholds)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ particle splitting (watershed)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ object classifier (interior brightness + shape)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ geometric/statistical markers (counting, lengths, distances)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ batch statistics (medians, bootstrap intervals)\\
  & $\rightarrow$ physics model (published DFN equations, PyBaMM)\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

If a reader disagrees with any step, they can change exactly that step ---
a threshold, a filter size, a parameter --- and re-run one command to see how
every downstream number responds.

Each image is a \emph{backscattered electron} (BSE) micrograph: brightness
corresponds loosely to average atomic number, so the three ingredients appear
as three brightness levels:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dark pixels} = pores (empty space).
  \item \textbf{Grey pixels} = graphite plus the carbon-binder matrix ---
  these cannot be told apart in BSE and are treated as one ``bulk'' phase
  throughout.
  \item \textbf{Bright pixels} = silicon-containing material.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Two framing limitations, stated up front.} First, the images do not
show the metal foil or the coating's outer surface, so we cannot measure total
electrode thickness from them, and we do not know with certainty which edge
faces the separator --- this is why directional markers are used as
\emph{ratios}, never as absolute directions. Second, at 25\,nm/pixel we
reliably resolve pores and particles down to about 0.35\,\textmu m; finer
structure exists but is invisible to us. We call the measured pore fraction
``resolved porosity'' and never equate it with true porosity.

\section{Method --- every step, explained}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Step 1 --- evening out the lighting}
SEM images often have a slow brightness gradient across the frame (the middle
is brighter than the corners). If we thresholded the raw image, a grey
graphite region in a bright corner could look like silicon.

The fix is called \emph{flat-field correction}: we blur a downsampled copy of
the image with a wide Gaussian filter, which captures only the slow lighting
gradient and none of the real particles, then divide the raw image by that
background. Afterwards, ``the same material'' has ``the same brightness'' in
every corner of the image --- which is the precondition for honest
thresholding.

\subsection{Step 2 --- colouring in the photograph (segmentation)}
We assign every pixel to pore, bulk or silicon using \emph{multi-Otsu
thresholding}: an algorithm that looks at the brightness histogram and finds
the two cut-off values that best separate the three brightness peaks.

\textbf{The single most important design choice:} the thresholds and the
object classifier are fitted \emph{once, on the 17 baseline (Batch\_3)
images only}, saved to \texttt{recipe.json}, and reused verbatim on every
later run ($t_{\text{pore}}=0.711$, $t_{\text{Si}}=1.335$,
$t_{\text{core}}=1.550$). If each photo were allowed its own thresholds, a
genuinely silicon-rich batch would simply re-calibrate itself into looking
normal --- like letting a suspect grade their own exam. Fitting on the
reference batch --- rather than all discovered images --- means a future
batch is measured against today's ruler, not a slightly moved one;
\texttt{run\_manifest.json} records the recipe hash, code hashes and every
input-image hash so each report identifies exactly which run produced it.
This freezes the \emph{software} ruler. Section~\ref{sec:acq} documents the
harder problem it cannot solve alone: the \emph{imaging} ruler --- session
noise, contrast and detector settings --- also varied between photographs,
and no software freeze can subtract a hardware drift after the fact.

After thresholding we remove specks below the resolution floor --- silicon
objects under 150~px and pores under 20~px (roughly 0.35\,\textmu m) are
absorbed into their surroundings --- so that single-pixel noise can never
become a ``particle''.

\subsection{Step 3 --- splitting touching particles (watershed)}
Silicon particles often touch in the image and get merged into one blob, which
would corrupt every particle-size statistic. We use the standard
\emph{watershed} algorithm: the image is treated as a landscape where distance
from the nearest non-silicon pixel is altitude; the landscape is ``flooded''
and ridges form where touching particles meet, splitting them at the natural
necks. This yields 4313 separate bright objects in total.

\subsection{Step 4 --- real silicon versus bright junk}
Not every bright object is a silicon particle. In low-contrast photographs,
stretched binder regions, particle edges and fines can pass the brightness
threshold but are not real particles. We therefore run an \emph{object-level
classifier}: for each bright object we measure its interior brightness
(ignoring its rim) and its compactness, and compare the interior brightness
against a cut-off $t_{\text{core}}=1.550$ derived from the 5th-percentile
interior brightness of large, obviously-real particles. Objects that fail are
labelled \texttt{bright\_fine} rather than \texttt{si\_particle}.

The split: \textbf{4313 silicon particles vs.\ 1714 bright-fine objects.}
Both populations are kept and reported separately (Figure~\ref{fig:overlays}),
so the reader can see exactly what the classifier did rather than trust it.
This matters: in the two flagged low-contrast photos the ``apparent silicon''
drops by roughly half after classification (raw bright fraction $\approx$10\%
$\rightarrow$ classified silicon $\approx$5\%) --- i.e.\ half the bright
material there was artefact. Had we not split it, those photos would have
silently doubled the measured silicon.

\subsection{Step 5 --- measuring the structure (the markers)}
From the three-colour map we compute roughly thirty \emph{markers} per image ---
single numbers that summarise one structural aspect. A reader with no
materials background only needs the intuition behind each group;
Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} defines every marker individually.

\subsubsection{Group A --- composition: ``how much of each ingredient?''}
The simplest possible measurement: count pixels of each colour. A classical
result called the \emph{Delesse principle} (1847) guarantees that, for a random
cut, the area fraction of a phase equals its volume fraction --- so counting
pixels on a slice legitimately estimates 3-D composition. We report
\texttt{pore\_frac\_resolved} (the pores we can see), \texttt{si\_frac\_total},
\texttt{gr\_frac}, and \texttt{bright\_fine\_frac} (the artefact budget ---
itself a data-quality guard).

\subsubsection{Group B --- silicon geometry: ``how big are the particles?''}
For every classified silicon particle we measure its area and shape, giving
the diameter distribution (\texttt{si\_d10/d50/d90}), the large-particle tail
(\texttt{si\_frac\_gt3um}, \texttt{\_gt5um}), solidity and aspect ratio.
The 3-D particle radius is corrected for the fact that a random slice rarely
cuts a sphere through its middle --- on average a 2-D section reads $\sim$21\%
small (the Wicksell/Saltykov correction, standard textbook stereology).
Surface area per volume (\texttt{si\_sv}) comes from the Crofton formula,
$S_V = \frac{4}{\pi} L_A$: total boundary length on the slice, rescaled.
Why it matters: silicon's surface is where the degrading SEI layer forms, and
particle size sets both the distance lithium must diffuse inside the particle
and the stress it feels on swelling.

\subsubsection{Group C --- graphite: ``how is the matrix arranged?''}
Same integral-geometric measures applied to the bulk--pore interface:
\texttt{gr\_sv} and an effective particle radius $R_{\text{eff}} = 3V/S$
(\texttt{gr\_radius\_eff\_um}). Graphite flakes are not spheres, so this is
honestly labelled an effective (diffusion-equivalent) radius. Mean horizontal
versus vertical chord lengths (\texttt{gr\_chord\_ratio\_xz}) reveal whether
flakes lie flat --- as they typically do after calendering.

\subsubsection{Group D --- pores and transport: ``how hard is the road for
lithium?''}
Pore size (\texttt{pore\_d50}), count density, and \emph{anisotropy}
(\texttt{pore\_anisotropy} $>1$ means pores lie flat, forcing ions onto longer
through-plane detours). Elongated near-vertical pores are counted separately as
\texttt{crack\_frac\_v} --- mechanical tears are also express ion lanes.

\textbf{Tortuosity} (how much longer a real path through the pores is than a
straight line) is attacked two ways. First, a geodesic proxy: the shortest
path through the pore maze versus a straight line, which feeds our
\emph{Bruggeman-equivalent exponent} \texttt{bruggeman\_b\_eff} (through
$\tau = \varepsilon^{1-b}$). Second, a true diffusion-solve tortuosity
(finite-difference Laplace solve through the pore phase, the same approach as
TauFactor/porespy). That second solver \emph{honestly reports
not-measurable} on every image: the resolved pore network never forms a
connected path across any 2-D section (\texttt{pore\_spans\_*} $=0$). This is
expected, not a bug --- through-plane connections leave the image plane, and
much of the real transport network lives in sub-resolution pores. We keep the
spanning check (\texttt{percolation\_reach\_2d},
\texttt{pore\_largest\_region\_frac}) as relative markers rather than inventing
finite numbers.

Finally, silicon accessibility: mean distance from each Si particle to the
nearest pore (\texttt{si\_pore\_dist\_mean\_um}), the share of Si fully
enclosed in bulk (\texttt{si\_enclosed\_share}), and per-particle pore
coverage of each Si boundary (\texttt{si\_lowcoverage\_share}). Silicon sealed
away from electrolyte-filled pores is effectively dead material.

\subsubsection{Group E --- swelling and heterogeneity: ``where will it
crack?''}
Silicon swells $\sim$280\% on lithiation. \texttt{swelling\_budget} asks,
locally, whether the nearby pore space can absorb each particle's expansion;
\texttt{swelling\_deficit\_area\_frac} is the fraction of the image where
demand exceeds supply --- a spatial risk map, not just an average.
\texttt{si\_clustering\_R} (Clark--Evans nearest-neighbour ratio, edge
corrected; $<1$ = clumped, $\approx1$ = random, $>1$ = evenly spread) and
\texttt{si\_patchiness} catch mixing problems that averages hide.

\subsubsection{Group F --- imaging guards}
Per-photo quality markers (\texttt{si\_bulk\_contrast}, noise, sharpness,
shading, detector presence, \texttt{is\_problem\_photo}) ensure an
``imaging'' difference cannot masquerade as a ``material'' difference.

\subsubsection{Group G --- assumed, never measured}
Electrode thickness (75\,\textmu m), the sub-resolution porosity correction
(+0.15--0.30), the Bruggeman default (1.5) and all material constants
(diffusivities, densities, kinetics --- from the published
Chen2020\_composite and OKane2022 parameter sets). These are declared
assumptions and are swept over, never baked in silently.

\subsection{Step 6 --- uncertainty: bands, not points}
Every marker is measured on every image, so a batch gives a small sample
(7, 7 or 17 values). We report the batch \emph{median} with its
interquartile range, and compare batches with a \emph{bootstrap} 95\%
confidence interval on the median difference: resample both batches' image
lists thousands of times, recompute the difference each time, and take the
central 95\% of the resulting distribution. A difference is called
\emph{clear} only when that interval excludes zero. This uses no assumption
about the data being normally distributed --- appropriate for small,
possibly skewed samples.

\subsection{Step 7 --- from images to a battery model (DFN)}
The Doyle--Fuller--Newman model is the standard physics model for lithium-ion
cells: coupled equations describing how lithium moves through electrolyte and
particles, how it reacts at surfaces, and how the cell voltage responds.
PyBaMM is a widely used open-source implementation. Crucially, DFN is
\emph{homogenised}: it does not want a 3-D picture; it wants a handful of
summary numbers --- which is exactly what our markers are. The mapping:

Because every input carries uncertainty, we do not run the model once. We run
a 9-point sweep per batch spanning the marker bands --- 27 solves in total ---
and report each output as a band, not a point. The model used is a composite
silicon/graphite DFN with particle swelling and cracking, SEI growth, and
stress-driven loss of active material enabled; three 1C charge--discharge
cycles per solve. Convergence was 27/27.

\subsection{The four candidate differences --- and the test they still
have to pass}
\label{sec:diffs}

Out of every marker $\times$ every batch pair, exactly four comparisons
survive the bootstrap test --- the 95\% confidence interval on the median
difference excludes zero. That number is itself informative: with $\sim$20
markers and 3 batch pairs, a handful of surviving differences means the
batches are \emph{mostly} alike. We originally reported these four as
``the differences''. They are better described as \emph{candidates},
because they were computed ignoring how the photographs were taken ---
and Section~\ref{sec:acq} shows why that omission matters. We keep them
here, fully analysed, because they are what any acquisition-controlled
re-test would need to re-establish.

All four point at \textbf{Batch\_1}. Two say its silicon sits differently;
two say its pore network is wired differently. Below, each difference is
expanded: what the marker is, how big the effect really is, what could
cause it in manufacturing, and why it matters.

\subsubsection{Difference 1 --- silicon is more clumped in Batch\_1
(Clark--Evans $R$: 0.73 vs.\ 0.80)}

\textbf{What it measures.} Pick every silicon particle's centre, and measure
how far each one is from its nearest neighbour. If particles were sprinkled
completely at random, the average nearest-neighbour distance takes a known
value; $R$ is the ratio of observed to expected. $R=1$ is random; $R>1$
means particles repel each other into an even grid; $R<1$ means they clump.
$R$ has a second intuitive reading: at $R=0.73$, a typical particle's nearest
neighbour is $\sim$27\% closer than random chance predicts.

\textbf{How big is the effect.} Batch\_1's median is $R=0.73$ --- every one
of its 7 photos sits in the range 0.70--0.81, i.e.\ \emph{consistently}
clumped. Batch\_2's median is 0.80 (range 0.73--0.94) --- some photos nearly
even. The medians differ by 9\%, and the bootstrap interval barely clears
zero, so the direction is reliable but the size is modest: we are talking
about visibly lumpier mixing, not separated islands. Crucially the
\emph{amount} of silicon is identical --- this is purely about where it
landed.

\textbf{Physical meaning.} Clumped silicon means clumped swelling. When
charging, each Si particle inflates to $\sim$3$\times$ its volume; a patch of
particles expanding together concentrates stress like several people pushing
on the same spot of a wall --- local cracking and delamination become more
likely than the average silicon content would suggest.

\textbf{Plausible manufacturing cause.} Mixing/dispersion differences ---
silicon aggregates that were not fully broken up, or slurry rheology that
let particles re-aggregate before drying. It is a \emph{process} signature,
not a recipe signature. Figure~\ref{fig:positions} makes it visible.

\subsubsection{Difference 2 --- Batch\_1's pore network reaches less far
(2-D percolation reach: 0.012 vs.\ 0.021)}

\textbf{What it measures.} Take every pore pixel that belongs to a connected
network spanning the image edge-to-edge, and ask what fraction of all pore
pixels that is. It is a coarse ``how wired-together is the pore maze''
score. (The stricter test --- a single unbroken path across the whole
section --- fails on \emph{all} images; see the tortuosity note in
Section~3.5.4.)

\textbf{How big is the effect.} Batch\_2's median reach is about
\textbf{1.8$\times$ Batch\_1's} (0.021 vs.\ 0.012). In plain terms, in a
typical Batch\_2 photo roughly twice as much of the visible pore space
belongs to a long-range connected channel as in Batch\_1. The batches
overlap at the tails, which is why the CI is wide, but the typical values
are nearly a factor of two apart.

\textbf{Physical meaning.} Pores are the electrolyte's roads. A
better-connected network means ions can reach deep into the coating through
fewer dead ends --- faster charging capability and more uniform utilisation.
Batch\_1's sparser resolved network implies more of its interior is served
only by sub-resolution pores we cannot see.

\textbf{Plausible cause / caveat.} Compaction (calendering) pressure,
particle packing, or simply which parts of the coating happened to be
sectioned. Honest caveat: this is a 2-D measurement of a 3-D network ---
real connectivity is certainly better than any section shows, and the
comparison is only meaningful \emph{between} batches measured identically.

\subsubsection{Difference 3 --- Batch\_3 has more vertical crack-like pores
(crack fraction: 0.012 vs.\ 0.0085)}

\textbf{What it measures.} The area share of pores that are both elongated
and oriented steeply --- the signature of mechanical tears running through
the coating's thickness rather than along it.

\textbf{How big is the effect.} Batch\_3's median is about \textbf{40\%
higher} than Batch\_1's (0.0119 vs.\ 0.0085) --- small in absolute terms
($\sim$1\% of image area) but consistent enough across images to clear the
test.

\textbf{Physical meaning --- genuinely ambiguous, and we say so.} Vertical
cracks cut both ways. As \emph{damage}, they are pre-existing failure
initiation sites --- bad. As \emph{ion lanes}, they are express routes that
short-circuit the tortuous pore maze --- arguably good for rate capability.
The data cannot choose between these readings; we report the difference and
its two-sided meaning rather than a verdict.

\textbf{Plausible cause.} Calendering damage, drying stress, or particle
fracture during pressing --- all of which are process history, not
composition.

\subsubsection{Difference 4 --- Batch\_1's largest pore region is smaller
(largest-region share: 0.045 vs.\ 0.065)}

\textbf{What it measures.} The single biggest connected pore blob, as a
fraction of all pore area. If the pore network were one big web, this would
approach 1; a fragmented network leaves it near zero.

\textbf{How big is the effect.} Batch\_3's typical largest pore region is
about \textbf{45\% bigger} than Batch\_1's. Same direction as difference~2,
measured a different way --- which is reassuring: two independent
connectivity markers agree that Batch\_1's resolved pore network is the most
fragmented. When two markers tell the same story, it is less likely to be
noise or a quirk of one particular definition.

\textbf{Physical meaning.} Again: electrolyte access. A fragmented network
has more isolated pockets --- regions ions can only reach through
connections too fine to see here.

\subsubsection{Reading the four candidates together --- unblocked}

Individually each candidate is modest. Together they sketch a coherent
story: \textbf{Batch\_1's structure looks spatially lumpier} --- silicon
gathered into patches, pores broken into more isolated pieces --- while
Batches 2 and 3 look smoother and better connected, with Batch\_3 carrying
slightly more (mechanical-history) vertical cracking. Every candidate is a
\emph{spatial arrangement} pattern; none is a \emph{composition}
difference. That pattern would point at process variables --- mixing,
coating, calendering --- \emph{if} it survives the imaging control test in
the next section.

\subsection{The acquisition confound --- when the camera is part of the
measurement}
\label{sec:acq}

\textbf{What we found while auditing our own conclusions.} The 31
photographs were not all taken under the same conditions. Grouping them by
frame size, pixel size, detector set, noise level and contrast reveals 13
distinct \emph{acquisition groups} --- inferred imaging sessions, since
real session metadata does not yet exist. The groups predict every
acquisition property almost perfectly (0--7\% residual variance for noise,
sharpness, bulk brightness and contrast), and they cut across the batch
labels: the approved reference, Batch\_3, appears in seven different
groups, and five groups contain photographs from more than one batch.

\textbf{Why this matters --- the measurement changes with the session.}
Table~\ref{tab:acq} partitions each feature's variance into ``how much is
left over after removing group means'' versus ``batch means''. For
\emph{count}-type markers the group nearly explains everything: within one
group, photographs from \emph{different} batches have almost identical pore
counts (4.3\% residual), while the same batch spread across sessions swings
by $\pm$20--50\%. For \emph{fraction}-type markers the opposite holds: batch
labels explain almost nothing, and real specimen-to-specimen spread shows
through even inside one imaging session (23\% residual).

\textbf{Why counts are fragile and fractions are not.} A count depends on
where object boundaries land, and boundary position shifts with acquisition
contrast and sharpness: a 16-pixel pore that blurs into two 8-pixel pores
doubles its contribution to the count while the total pore \emph{area} is
conserved. That is why a minimum-size cut-off did not rescue the count ---
the session effect is not tiny specks at the detection floor, it is edge
drift across the whole size range. Area fractions are merge-invariant by
construction. The practical consequence for any future analysis:
\emph{fractions are primary markers; counts are within-session diagnostics
only}.

\textbf{The blocking test --- what survives.} We re-ran the batch
comparisons using only photographs taken inside the five groups that mix
batches (a stratified permutation that shuffles batch labels only within
groups, after centring each marker on its group median). The available
evidence is thin --- Batch\_1 and Batch\_3 share exactly one group, with one
photo each --- but the result is unambiguous in direction:

\textbf{Does the pipeline see material or sessions?} Two more checks make
the picture sharper. (a)~A classifier given only acquisition information ---
frame height, noise, contrast --- predicts the batch label with 68\%
leave-one-out accuracy, \emph{above} the 55\% majority baseline; the full
image-feature set manages only 42\%. The imaging conditions carry more
apparent batch information than the material features do. (b)~The three
new-image assignments each landed in the one batch that shares the photo's
acquisition group --- consistent with either a correct batch match or pure
session matching; the blind protocol was correct, but the outcome cannot
distinguish the two.

\textbf{What this does and does not prove.} It does not prove the batches
are identical --- the within-session test is severely under-powered (the
key Batch\_1--Batch\_3 pair shares a session exactly once). It proves that
the current dataset cannot separate material differences from acquisition
conditions, and therefore that no batch-comparison claim from count-type or
unblocked markers is presently supportable. The definitive fix is in the
measurement system: acquisition metadata from the imaging lab (dates,
voltage, working distance, detector settings, specimen IDs) to convert the
13 inferred groups into confirmed sessions, or ideally a re-imaging run
with batches interleaved within each session.

\subsubsection{How to read Table~\ref{tab:dfn} and
Figure~\ref{fig:bands}}

Each number is a median over 17 model runs spanning the marker uncertainty
band, and every batch is evaluated with the \emph{same} cathode at each
sweep index --- so batch differences are read as paired differences at
fixed assumptions, not as median gaps. The rows, in plain terms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cycle-5 discharge capacity ($\sim$5.5--5.7\,Ah).}
  Predicted output after five 1C cycles. Paired differences (+0.11\,Ah for
  B1$-$B3, +0.15\,Ah for B2$-$B3, $-0.05$\,Ah for B1$-$B2) all sit below
  the 0.25\,Ah threshold declared before the runs --- and the swept
  assumptions alone move the answer by $\sim$1.14\,Ah (IQR). Below
  threshold, honestly reported.
  \item \textbf{Peak cell thickness change ($\sim$12\,\textmu m during
  discharge).} The \emph{maximum} swelling reached mid-cycle, not the
  relaxed end-state --- an earlier version of this analysis read the wrong
  moment and saw nothing. Paired differences ($\le$0.15\,\textmu m) stay
  below the declared 0.5\,\textmu m threshold.
  \item \textbf{Peak silicon surface stress (9.5--10.8\,MPa during
  discharge).} The one comparison that \emph{exceeds} its declared
  threshold (0.5\,MPa) on all three pairs: B2$-$B3 $=-1.60$\,MPa
  ($-15\%$ of the B3 value), B1$-$B3 $=-0.95$\,MPa ($-9\%$), B1$-$B2
  $=+0.53$\,MPa ($+5\%$), with paired confidence bands excluding zero.
  Inside the model's physics this ordering is driven almost entirely by
  the \emph{modelled} share of electrically reachable silicon
  (Spearman $\rho\approx-0.93$ with \texttt{si\_accessible\_frac}); silicon
  particle size contributes nothing ($\rho\approx0$). Two caveats are
  mandatory: the carrier input is a 2-D contact proxy, not measured
  connectivity, and the comparison inherits the acquisition confound ---
  every input marker came from the same session-entangled images.
  \item \textbf{Minimum surface potential ($-8$ to $-9$\,mV; plating
  context).} Every run of every batch dips slightly below 0\,V anode
  surface potential during charge. Under the corrected rule --- a warning
  counts only where the reference cell stays above zero under the same
  protocol --- this is attributed to the shared assumed geometry
  (75\,\textmu m thickness, generic kinetics), not to any batch. Kept as a
  model-diagnostic, excluded from the batch verdict.
  \item \textbf{Lithium loss, retention, Coulombic efficiency ---
  diagnostics only.} Nothing ages measurably in five cycles (retention
  $\approx$100\%, LLI $\approx$0.01\%, CE $\approx$0.9995 in all batches),
  so these are reported but explicitly \emph{not compared}. An earlier
  pipeline version compared them anyway --- and multiplied the lithium
  loss by a factor of $\sim$3600 through a units bug. Both errors are
  recorded rather than hidden.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What the model adds --- and what it cannot do.} The DFN is a
consequence model: it translates measured inputs into physical responses
and adds no measurement of its own. Its one defensible contribution is the
stress ordering above --- a hypothesis, in plain words, that ``Batch\_2's
silicon may sit in a slightly more electrically accessible configuration
than Batch\_3's, which would spread lithiation over more particles and
lower per-particle stress''. The input markers that carry it are each
individually below significance; the model integrates several weak,
correlated shifts through a steep response. That makes it a signal worth
testing directly in the image layer --- not a measured fact, and not a
batch verdict.

\section{What the results mean, physically}
%=====================================================================

\subsection{Five insights, in plain terms}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Composition is indistinguishable --- the only
  batch-comparative claim that survives.} Silicon-candidate fraction
  (5.95 / 6.14 / 6.03\%), resolved pore fraction ($\sim$10\%), particle
  size and surface area all overlap. The honest phrasing is ``no
  detectable difference'': with seven photographs per batch, gaps under
  roughly two area-points would be invisible. The earlier ``excess
  silicon'' alarm was a threshold disagreement between two measurement
  recipes, not material --- and the three-label ledger keeps it that way.

  \item \textbf{Three Batch\_1 fields carry unusually high bright-material
  area --- real as an image observation, unresolved as material.} Three
  of seven Batch\_1 photos hold 10.7--12.5\% segmented bright area against
  the Batch\_3 median of 7.7\%. Two of the three are the low-contrast
  fields where much of that material is classified \emph{uncertain bright}
  rather than candidate silicon --- and in the one session where a flagged
  field shares a group with a Batch\_2 photo, the two are nearly identical
  (10.7\% vs.\ 10.3\%). Solid as an observation of what is in the images;
  ``can't tell'' as a batch claim until acquisition and chemistry are
  resolved.

  \item \textbf{The camera is part of the measurement --- the study's own
  central finding.} Section~\ref{sec:acq} is not a caveat, it is a result:
  measured pore \emph{counts} organise almost perfectly by imaging group
  (4\% residual) and barely by batch (53\%), while area \emph{fractions}
  carry the specimen signal. The robust-measurement lesson --- report
  fractions, demote counts --- is a methodological result any comparable
  pipeline can reuse. Every unblocked batch candidate, including the four
  in Section~\ref{sec:diffs}, collapses under within-session blocking;
  the test is under-powered (the key pair shares one session), so the
  correct label is ``can't tell'', not ``identical''.

  \item \textbf{Every batch sits in a quantified swelling deficit --- a
  dataset-level finding immune to the confound.} Silicon swells
  $\sim$2.8$\times$ on lithiation, and in most 12.5\,\textmu m windows the
  resolved pore space cannot absorb that expansion; only
  $\sim$1.5--2.1\% of candidate-particle boundary lies within 75\,nm of a
  resolved pore. For a dense calendered coating this is normal --- but it
  means the silicon's electrolyte supply runs through sub-resolution
  porosity the images cannot see. Stated as a measured blind spot, not a
  defect: boundary contact, wetting and electrochemical activity are
  different quantities. Figure~\ref{fig:deficit} shows the deficit tiles
  tracking the silicon.

  \item \textbf{The widest error bar is still upstream of everything.}
  The sensitivity analysis ranks assumed true porosity far above every
  measured input (assumptions move predicted capacity by $\sim$1.14\,Ah
  against $\le$0.15\,Ah of paired batch difference), and the
  label-uncertainty bracket --- classified silicon versus all
  uncertain-bright counted --- alone moves the stress answer by up to
  1.8\,MPa, comparable to the batch stress difference itself. The two
  highest-value next measurements are acquisition metadata (to break the
  session--batch tangle) and a measured porosity; both beat more photos.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The exploratory detector layer --- what InLens and ETD added}
\label{sec:channels}

Every photograph also carries two surface-sensitive detector channels
(InLens and ETD), perfectly co-registered with the BSE frame (0-pixel
shift on all 34 fields). We deliberately keep them \emph{out} of the
frozen segmentation --- surface topography is a different physical
quantity than atomic-number contrast, and their acquisition settings
differ by batch --- but as an exploratory layer they answered three
questions BSE alone could not:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pore contrast (label: possible).} Batch\_1's pores read
  darkest in InLens in all three sessions it shares with another batch ---
  the only candidate signal whose direction survived blocking. With $n=3$
  matched observations and InLens gain itself differing by batch
  (median brightness $\approx$89 for Batch\_3 vs.\ $\sim$110 for
  Batches~1--2), this is a follow-up signal for controlled re-imaging,
  not a measured pore depth.
  \item \textbf{The ``uncertain-bright'' label is doing real work.} The
  material the classifier refuses to call silicon is at-or-above
  classified-silicon brightness in both surface channels, with about twice
  the surface texture --- thin, edge-rich bright material rather than bulk
  grains. That validates keeping it as its own label; it does not prove it
  is silicon (edge and topography also read bright in surface channels),
  so the upper bracket remains a bracket.
  \item \textbf{A strong vertical texture exists in every image}
  (autocorrelation lengths $\sim$3--5$\times$ longer along the vertical
  axis, plus Fourier agreement) and InLens does not exaggerate it ---
  arguing against it being a pure surface-relief artefact. Its origin
  (calendering texture, or something else) remains open.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the model can and cannot say about the batches}

Worth stating plainly: on three of four indicators the batches are
indistinguishable, and on the fourth --- peak silicon stress --- the model
reports a real, threshold-exceeding ordering that is nonetheless
\emph{conditional} twice over: on the model's physics (which weights
reachable-silicon share heavily, $\rho\approx-0.93$), and on image inputs
that are themselves acquisition-entangled. The honest reading is a
hypothesis to take back to the image layer --- ``Batch\_2's silicon may be
in a more accessible configuration'' --- not a verdict. The bands,
meanwhile, tell the durable truth: assumption and label uncertainty move
every indicator more than the batches do.

\subsection{What this analysis did detect, and what it would take to go
further}

The pipeline caught: a compositional identity result with explicit
detection bounds; three unusually bright Batch\_1 fields (real as an
observation, unresolved as material); a systematic imaging artefact that
would have doubled apparent silicon in two photos; a universal
swelling-deficit regime; a count-versus-fraction measurement lesson;
and --- most importantly --- the acquisition confound itself, quantified
rather than suspected. What it could not detect: any batch difference
separable from imaging session. The highest-leverage next steps, in order:
(1)~acquisition metadata or an interleaved re-imaging run --- converts the
confound from ``can't tell'' into a controlled variable; (2)~a measured
electrode porosity and thickness (removes the widest model band);
(3)~EDS on a reviewed subset (converts ``bright'' into a chemical name);
(4)~electrochemical ground truth, a few cells per batch, to calibrate the
indicators against reality.

\section{Limitations --- what we cannot claim}
%=====================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Acquisition and material are not adequately separated ---
  the dominant limitation.} The inferred imaging groups are unevenly spread
  across batches, and the key Batch\_1--Batch\_3 pair shares a session
  exactly once. No batch-difference claim is currently supportable from
  count-type or unblocked markers; ``can't tell'' is the correct status,
  not ``identical''. The groups themselves are inferred (frame size, pixel
  size, noise, contrast) and need session metadata for confirmation.
  \item \textbf{``Bright'' is not ``silicon''.} BSE contrast identifies
  atomic-number-bright material; it cannot distinguish silicon from
  silicon-oxide-rich phases, edge artefacts or other heavy phases. EDS on
  a reviewed subset is the gate, and every silicon-fraction number in this
  report carries that asterisk.
  \item \textbf{Absolute values are illustrative; batch differences are the
  defensible signal.} We resolve only $\approx$10\% porosity against a true
  25--40\%; thickness is assumed; material constants are generic literature
  values. All three are swept, but they still cap absolute accuracy.
  \item \textbf{No 3-D connectivity.} The resolved pore network does not
  span any 2-D section; true through-plane tortuosity would need tomography
  or a reconstruction branch (SliceGAN + TauFactor), treated as an optional
  sensitivity analysis, not ground truth.
  \item \textbf{BSE cannot see} binder distribution, the SEI layer, lithium
  inventory, electrolyte wetting, or foil corrosion.
  \item \textbf{Model outputs are indicators under stated assumptions,}
  not validated predictions of capacity, cycle life or failure. They
  inherit the acquisition confound through their inputs, and there is no
  electrochemical ground truth in this dataset.
  \item \textbf{Statistical power} is limited by image counts (7/7/17) and,
  more sharply, by the near-total lack of shared imaging sessions between
  batches --- the binding constraint on every within-session test.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}
%=====================================================================

The frozen recipe is fitted once on the baseline batch and reused
verbatim; every run writes a manifest identifying the exact ruler, code and
inputs that produced it:

\begin{verbatim}
.venv/bin/python run_dfn.py --baseline Batch_3 --out dfn_output  # fit+freeze
.venv/bin/python run_dfn.py --recipe recipe.json --reuse         # cached rerun
.venv/bin/python analyze_channels.py                             # InLens/ETD layer
.venv/bin/python analyze_spatial.py                              # spatial markers
\end{verbatim}

\texttt{dfn\_output/} contains: \texttt{report.md} (readable summary),
\texttt{markers\_*.csv} (per image), \texttt{objects\_*.csv} (per detected
object), \texttt{batch\_summary.csv}, \texttt{dfn\_paired\_differences.csv}
(every pair-indicator verdict), \texttt{recipe.json} (frozen cut-offs),
\texttt{run\_manifest.json} (recipe/code/image hashes), and
\texttt{dfn\_params\_*.json} (exact simulator inputs per run).
The acquisition audit produces \texttt{image\_acquisition.csv} (one row per
photograph: frame size, pixel size, percentiles, noise, brightness,
contrast, group ID) plus the variance-partition and blocking tables quoted
in Section~\ref{sec:acq}; the detector-layer audit produces
\texttt{channel\_features.csv} and \texttt{channel\_report.md}.
